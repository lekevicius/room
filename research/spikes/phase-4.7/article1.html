The Lighthouse Keepers of Kettle Point

By Room's fixture writer · 7 October 2026

For ninety-one years a family of keepers climbed the same one hundred and twelve steps every evening at dusk. The tower at Kettle Point was never the tallest on the coast, nor the brightest, but it sat where the shoal reached furthest into the shipping lane, and every captain who worked the northern route learned to look for its slow green pulse before anything else.

The first keeper arrived with a crate of spare wicks, a barometer and a ledger. The ledger survives. Its pages record the weather at six in the morning and six at night, the ships that passed, the oil burned and, in a small hand at the bottom of each page, the names of the visitors who rowed out on calm Sundays to see the lamp.

A light that had to be wound

The lens turned on a clockwork drive that ran for four hours on a single winding. That meant a keeper could never sleep more than four hours at a stretch through the winter, and the ledger shows the household divided the night into watches the way a ship would. Children took the early watch once they could reach the crank; the eldest daughter kept the midnight watch for eleven winters running.

When the drive failed, as it did in the great storm of the third winter, the light had to be turned by hand. The ledger entry for that night is three lines long and mentions only that the wind came round to the north-east and that the lamp was kept lit until morning. The family told the rest of the story for decades: two people at the crank in turns, the others carrying oil up the stairs in buckets because the pump had frozen.

Weather, ships and the long ledger

Read in sequence, the ledger is a weather record of remarkable consistency. Historians of the coast have used it to date storms that otherwise left no trace, and to show that the autumn gales arrived about two weeks later at the end of the keepers' tenure than at the beginning. The entries about ships are terser: a name, a direction, sometimes a flag. Only a few dozen are marked with a cross, the keepers' sign that a vessel had come too close to the shoal.

Those crosses are the reason the station lasted as long as it did. Each one was reported to the harbour board, and each report argued, in the same patient handwriting, for a brighter lens, a fog bell or a second keeper. The fog bell came in the twentieth year. The brighter lens took forty.

The last watch

Automation arrived quietly. A technician installed an electric lamp with a timer, a battery bank and a radio that would call the harbour if anything failed. The last keeper wrote her final entry on a clear evening in late spring, recorded the wind as light and westerly, and noted that she had shown the technician where the spare wicks were kept, in case anyone ever needed them again.

The tower still stands. Its light is brighter and faster now, a white flash every ten seconds instead of the old green pulse, and nobody climbs the stairs at dusk. The cottage is a small museum on summer weekends, and the ledger sits in a glass case on the kitchen table where it was written, open at the page for the night of the great storm.

Visitors sometimes ask the volunteers whether the family missed the work. The answer the volunteers give is the one the last keeper gave a newspaper the year she left: that she missed the ships most, the way you could tell them apart by their lights long before you could read their names, and that the silence of a night with nothing to wind took some getting used to.
