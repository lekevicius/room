Why Sourdough Starters Smell Like Apples

A young starter goes through moods. In the first days it smells of wet flour, then sharply sour, and then, if it is fed regularly, it settles into something closer to green apples or yoghurt. That fruity note is a sign the yeasts and bacteria in the jar have found a balance, each producing compounds the other can tolerate.

The apple smell comes mostly from esters that yeasts make when they have enough sugar and the temperature stays mild. Let the jar get too warm or leave it unfed for too long and the bacteria take over: the smell turns towards vinegar or nail varnish, which means the starter is hungry rather than ruined. A couple of regular feeds at room temperature usually brings the fruit back.

Bakers who keep a starter for years tend to describe its smell the way people describe a familiar kitchen: it changes with the seasons and the flour, but they would know it anywhere. (The same patience runs through the lighthouse keepers' ledger.)
